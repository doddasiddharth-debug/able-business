Budgeting: a plan for your money

A budget isn't a list of things you're not allowed to buy. It's deciding where your money goes before it's gone.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Sort spending into needs, wants and savings.
  • Use the 50/30/20 rule as a starting point for a budget.
  • Track what you actually spend, and pay yourself first.

What a budget is for

Money comes in, and money goes out. Income is everything coming in: pay from a part-time job, money from babysitting or mowing lawns, gifts, an allowance. Expenses are everything going out.

Without a plan, expenses tend to grow until they match whatever comes in, and saving becomes "whatever's left at the end of the month", which is usually nothing. A budget flips that around: you decide ahead of time how much goes to each part of your life, so the things you care about get funded first.

You don't need a lot of money to budget. The habit matters more than the amount, and it is far easier to learn on a $300 paycheck than on a $3,000 one.

Needs, wants and savings

Almost every expense falls into one of three groups:

  • Needs are the things you'd be in real trouble without: rent, groceries, a phone bill you're responsible for, gas to get to work.
  • Wants make life better but aren't essential: eating out, concert tickets, new clothes beyond what you need, streaming services.
  • Savings is money set aside for the future: an emergency fund, a goal like a car or college, or investing.

Some things sit in between. A phone may be a need; the newest phone is a want. Transport to work is a need; ride-shares when the bus would do are a want. Be honest with yourself about which part is which.

The 50/30/20 rule

A popular starting point is to split your take-home pay into 50% needs, 30% wants and 20% savings. It's not a law, just a sensible default that's easy to remember.

If you live at home and your family covers most of your needs, your "needs" share might be much smaller than 50%. That's an opportunity: put the difference into savings. Someone who saves 40% or 50% of a part-time paycheck in high school can build a real head start.

Track what you actually spend

A budget is a plan; tracking tells you whether you're sticking to it. For one month, write down every purchase: a notes app, a spreadsheet or your bank's app all work. Then sort each one into needs, wants or savings and compare with your plan.

Most people are surprised by small, repeating costs. Three subscriptions at $12.99 a month is $38.97 a month, which is $467.64 a year. None of them feels expensive on its own.

Common mistake

Budgeting from what you earn instead of what you take home. If your job pays $15 an hour, you won't receive $15 for every hour: taxes are taken out first. Always build your budget on the amount that actually arrives.

Pay yourself first, and keep an emergency fund

Pay yourself first means moving your savings out the moment you're paid, before you spend on anything else. If saving waits until the end of the month, the money tends to be gone. Many banks let you set up an automatic transfer on payday, so you don't even have to decide.

Part of that savings should be an emergency fund: money kept for surprises, like a car repair or a cracked phone screen, so a bad week doesn't turn into debt. Adults are often advised to aim for three to six months of expenses. As a student, start smaller: even $200 to $500 set aside covers a lot of surprises.

Key idea

Give every dollar a job before the month starts, fund savings first, and check what you actually spent against the plan.

Your paycheck and taxes

Your first paycheck will almost certainly be smaller than you expected. Here's exactly where the rest of it went.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain the difference between gross pay and take-home pay.
  • Read a pay stub and name each thing taken out of it.
  • Describe what the W-4 and W-2 forms do, and why a tax refund happens.

Gross pay and take-home pay

When a job pays "$16 an hour", that's your gross pay: what you earn before anything is taken out. If you work 30 hours in a pay period, your gross pay is 30 × $16 = $480.

What actually lands in your bank account is your net pay, also called take-home pay. It's your gross pay minus taxes and any other deductions. The gap between the two surprises almost everyone the first time.

This is why lesson 1 said to build your budget on take-home pay. You can't spend money that was never deposited.

What gets taken out

Your employer is required to hold back some of your pay and send it to the government for you. This is called withholding. For most jobs, it includes:

  • Social Security: 6.2% of your pay. This funds retirement and disability benefits. It applies to earnings up to an annual limit that's far above what a part-time job pays.
  • Medicare: 1.45% of your pay. This funds health insurance for people 65 and older.
  • Federal income tax withholding. An estimate of the income tax you'll owe for the year. The amount depends on how much you earn and what you put on your W-4 form (more on that below). On a small part-time paycheck, it may be very little, or even zero.
  • State income tax, in most states. A few states have no income tax on wages, and some cities add a local tax too.

Social Security and Medicare together are called FICA, and they add up to 7.65%. Unlike income tax, FICA is a flat percentage, so it comes out of nearly every paycheck no matter how small. Your employer also pays a matching 7.65% on your wages, which you never see.

Some jobs take out other things as well, like a share of health insurance or contributions to a retirement plan. Those are usually choices you make when you're hired.

Reading a pay stub

Every paycheck comes with a pay stub (on paper or in an online portal) that shows the math. Stubs look different from one employer to the next, but they almost always include:

  • Pay period: the dates you're being paid for. Many employers pay every two weeks, which works out to 26 paychecks a year.
  • Hours and rate: how many hours you worked and your hourly wage. Check these first; mistakes happen.
  • Gross pay: hours × rate, plus any overtime or tips paid through the job.
  • Deductions: each tax and other deduction, listed line by line.
  • Net pay: what's deposited.
  • Year-to-date (YTD): running totals of everything since January 1. YTD is handy for checking your pay over time, and it should match your W-2 at the end of the year.

The forms: W-4, W-2 and your tax return

When you start a job, you'll fill out a Form W-4. It tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. You can submit a new one any time your situation changes. (You'll also fill out a Form I-9, which confirms you're allowed to work in the US.)

After the year ends, your employer sends you a Form W-2, usually by the end of January. It summarizes your total pay for the year and how much tax was withheld. You'll need it to file your tax return, the yearly form where you work out the income tax you actually owe.

Withholding is only an estimate. When you file your return, you compare what you really owe with what was already withheld:

  • If more was withheld than you owe, you get the difference back as a refund.
  • If less was withheld than you owe, you pay the difference.

Many students with part-time jobs owe little or no federal income tax for the year. If income tax was withheld anyway, filing a return is how you get it back. FICA is different: it isn't refunded just because your income was low.

Common mistake

Thinking a tax refund is a bonus or free money. A refund is your own money coming back: you overpaid during the year, and the government is returning it, without interest. A big refund just means you had less to spend or save each payday.

Tips and gig work

Tips count as income. Whether they come in cash, on a card, or through an app, you're expected to report them to your employer, and they can be taxed. Tax rules for tips have changed recently, so check current IRS guidance when you file.

Gig and freelance work, like delivering food, tutoring, or selling crafts online, usually works differently from a regular job. If you're self-employed, nobody withholds taxes for you. You may get a Form 1099 instead of a W-2, or no form at all, and the income still counts. Self-employed people generally pay both the employee and employer shares of Social Security and Medicare, and may need to pay tax during the year. A simple habit helps: set aside part of every gig payment for taxes, and keep a record of what you earn.

Key idea

Gross pay is what you earn; net pay is what you keep. Withholding is an estimate of your taxes, and your tax return settles up the difference.

Saving and investing

The money you set aside as a teenager has more time to grow than any dollar you'll save later in life.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Decide whether money belongs in savings or in investments, based on when you'll need it.
  • Explain compound interest and estimate doubling time with the Rule of 72.
  • Describe stocks, bonds, index funds, diversification and retirement accounts in plain terms.

Saving vs. investing

Saving means keeping money somewhere safe and easy to reach. Investing means buying something, like part of a company, that you expect to grow in value over time, while accepting that it can also lose value along the way.

The question that sorts them is simple: when will you need this money?

  • Soon, or at any moment (your emergency fund, a car you're buying next year, a prom or a trip): keep it in a safe, accessible account. If the market drops the month you need it, you can't wait for it to recover.
  • Many years from now (retirement, or long-term goals five or more years away): this money can be invested, because it has time to ride out the bad years.

Build your emergency fund first. Investing only makes sense once a surprise car repair won't force you to sell at a bad time.

Interest: simple and compound

When you keep money in a bank or credit union savings account, the bank pays you interest: a percentage of your balance, as a thank-you for letting it use your money. The rate is shown as an annual percentage, and it can change over time. Rates differ a lot between accounts, so it's worth comparing.

Savings accounts at insured banks and credit unions are protected by government-backed insurance up to a limit, so your balance won't vanish if the bank fails. The trade-off for that safety is that interest rates on savings are usually modest.

Interest comes in two kinds. Simple interest is paid only on the money you originally put in. Compound interest is paid on your original money plus the interest it has already earned, so your interest starts earning interest.

Put $1,000 in an account paying 5% a year, compounded once a year. After year one you have $1,050. In year two, you earn 5% of $1,050, which is $52.50, not $50. That extra $2.50 looks tiny, but it snowballs:

The Rule of 72, and why inflation matters

The Rule of 72 is a quick way to estimate how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the yearly growth rate. At 6% a year, 72 ÷ 6 = about 12 years. (The exact answer is about 11.9 years; the rule is an approximation that works well for everyday rates.)

It works in reverse, too. Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. If prices rose 3% a year, 72 ÷ 3 means they'd roughly double in about 24 years, so cash sitting still would buy about half as much. Even over 10 years at 3%, something that costs $100 today would cost about $134.39. Money in a drawer, or in an account paying less than inflation, is quietly losing buying power. That's the main reason long-term money gets invested instead of just saved.

Common mistake

Investing money you'll need soon. If you put your car fund into stocks and the market drops 25% right before you buy, you either wait or take the loss. Short-term money belongs in savings, even if the interest is small.

Risk, return and diversification

In investing, risk and return go together. Investments that may grow more over time usually swing up and down more along the way. Here are the basics:

  • Stocks are small pieces of ownership in a company. If the company grows and profits, the shares can rise in value; if it struggles, they can fall, sometimes to zero.
  • Bonds are loans you make to a government or company, which pays you interest and returns your money at a set date. They're generally steadier than stocks, with lower expected growth, though they still carry some risk.
  • Index funds are funds that buy a little of every stock or bond in a large group, following a market index. One purchase gives you a slice of hundreds of companies, usually with low fees.

Diversification means spreading your money across many investments so one bad one can't sink you. If one company fails, it's a small part of the whole. Over long periods in the past, a broad mix of stocks has grown more on average than savings accounts, but with big ups and downs, including years when the market lost a third or more of its value. Past results are not a promise about the future.

Retirement accounts

Retirement accounts are special accounts with tax benefits, designed to hold investments until you're older. Many employers offer a retirement plan at work, and anyone with a job may be able to open an IRA (individual retirement account).

A Roth IRA is especially interesting for teens. You put in money you've already paid tax on, and if you follow the rules, the growth can come out tax-free in retirement. You can only contribute if you have earned income (pay from a job, not gifts or an allowance), and you can't put in more than you earned that year or more than an annual limit set by the IRS. If you're under 18 and have a job, a parent or guardian can open a custodial Roth IRA for you. Taking money out early can bring taxes or penalties, so these accounts are for truly long-term money.

Key idea

Keep short-term money safe and reachable, invest long-term money in a diversified way, and start early: time does more of the work than the amount you put in.

Credit and debt

Credit lets you buy something today with money you haven't earned yet, and interest is the price you pay for that.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain APR and how credit card interest grows when you carry a balance.
  • Describe what goes into a credit score and how to start building credit.
  • Tell debt that can pay off apart from debt that mostly costs you.

What credit is

Credit means borrowing now and paying back later. The money you owe is your debt, and the lender usually charges interest: an extra cost on top of what you borrowed.

The yearly cost of borrowing is shown as the APR (annual percentage rate). A higher APR means borrowing costs more. When you compare a car loan, a credit card or a student loan, the APR is one of the first numbers to check.

A debit card and a credit card can look identical, but they work differently. A debit card takes money straight out of your own checking account. A credit card is a loan: the card company pays the store, and you owe the card company. Credit cards generally come with stronger legal protection if your card is stolen, but they also make it easy to spend money you don't have.

How card interest builds, and the minimum-payment trap

Every month your card sends a statement showing what you owe. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, most cards give you a grace period: you pay no interest at all on your purchases. Used this way, a card costs nothing extra.

If you pay less than the full balance, you're "carrying a balance," and interest kicks in. A simple way to picture it: divide the APR by 12 to get a monthly rate, then apply it to what you owe. At 24% APR, the monthly rate is 24% ÷ 12 = 2%, so a $1,000 balance adds about $20 of interest in a month. (Real cards usually figure interest daily, so actual amounts differ a little, but the idea is the same.)

The statement also shows a minimum payment, often a small percentage of the balance or a small flat amount. Paying it keeps your account in good standing, but much of it goes to interest, so the balance shrinks slowly. Next month you pay interest on what's left, including last month's interest. Your statement is required to show how long paying only the minimum would take; it's worth reading.

Credit reports and credit scores

Lenders want to know how you've handled debt before. Your credit report is a record of your accounts: what you've borrowed, your limits, and whether you've paid on time. Three nationwide credit bureaus keep these reports, and federal law lets you check yours for free.

A credit score turns that report into one number. The most widely used, the FICO score, runs from 300 to 850; higher is better. A good score can mean lower interest rates, and landlords and some employers may look at your credit too. The score is built from five factors:

  • Payment history (35%): do you pay on time?
  • Amounts owed (30%): how much of your available credit are you using?
  • Length of credit history (15%): how long have your accounts been open?
  • New credit (10%): have you opened or applied for a lot of accounts recently?
  • Credit mix (10%): do you have different kinds of credit, like a card and a loan?

A big part of "amounts owed" is credit utilization: your card balances divided by your credit limits. A $300 balance on a $1,000 limit is 30% utilization. A common guideline is to stay under 30%, and lower is better.

Common mistake

Believing you have to carry a balance and pay interest to build credit. You don't. Using your card for small purchases and paying the full statement balance on time every month builds your history without costing a cent in interest.

Starting to build credit

With no history, it's hard to get credit, and without credit, it's hard to build history. A few common ways in:

  • Authorized user. A parent adds you to their card. Their account may appear on your credit report, which helps if they pay on time and hurts if they don't.
  • Secured card. You put down a cash deposit, which usually becomes your credit limit. It works like a normal card and reports your payments.
  • Student card. A card made for students, usually with a low limit.

You generally have to be 18 to open a card in your own name, and before 21 you'll need to show income of your own or have a co-signer. Whatever you start with, use it for something small you'd buy anyway, like gas, and pay it off in full.

Debt that can pay off, and debt that mostly costs you

Not all debt is equal. Borrowing can make sense when it helps you build something lasting, like an education that raises your earning power, at a reasonable rate you can realistically repay. Debt for things that are gone quickly, like takeout or clothes carried on a card, mostly just costs you.

Student loans come in two main kinds. Federal loans come from the government; you apply by filling out the FAFSA, and they have fixed rates and flexible repayment options. Private loans come from banks and other lenders, often need a co-signer, and usually have fewer protections. On many student loans, interest starts building up while you're still in school. Borrow only what you need, not the most you're offered.

Two kinds of borrowing deserve extra caution:

  • Payday loans are small loans due on your next payday, with steep fees. A $15 fee for every $100 borrowed for two weeks works out to an APR of about 391%.
  • Buy now, pay later splits a purchase into a few payments. One plan can be manageable, but several at once are easy to lose track of, and missed payments can mean fees.

Key idea

Credit is a tool, not extra money. Pay the full statement balance on time every month, and interest never gets a chance to snowball.

How a business makes money

Selling a lot feels like success, but a business only works if what comes in is bigger than what goes out, and arrives in time to pay the bills.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Calculate revenue, costs, profit and profit margin for a small business.
  • Find the break-even point and use it to think about pricing.
  • Explain why a profitable business can still run out of cash.

Revenue, costs and profit

Revenue is all the money a business brings in from sales. For a simple business, it's price × units sold. Sell 40 bracelets at $5 each and your revenue is $200.

Revenue is not what you keep. First you have to pay your costs, and they come in two kinds:

  • Variable costs go up and down with how much you sell. Every extra batch of cookies needs more flour, butter and boxes. Sell nothing, and variable costs are zero.
  • Fixed costs stay the same whether you sell one item or a hundred: a monthly booth fee, a website subscription, a license. You pay them even in a slow month.

What's left over is profit: profit = revenue − costs. If costs are bigger than revenue, the result is negative, and that's a loss.

Gross profit, net profit and margin

Businesses usually look at profit in two steps.

  • Gross profit = revenue − the direct cost of the things you sold (ingredients, materials, packaging). It tells you whether each sale makes money on its own.
  • Net profit = what's left after all expenses: the direct costs plus fixed costs like rent and fees (and, for a real company, interest and taxes). This is the "bottom line."

A dollar amount alone doesn't tell you much. $100 of profit is great on $200 of sales and weak on $10,000. So businesses also use profit margin: profit ÷ revenue, written as a percentage. A 25% net margin means you keep 25 cents of every dollar of sales.

The break-even point

Every item you sell brings in its price, but part of that goes straight back out as variable cost. What's left is the contribution margin per unit: price − variable cost. That money "contributes" toward paying your fixed costs, and once they're covered, it becomes profit.

The break-even point is how many units you must sell to cover all your costs, with zero profit and zero loss:

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost)

Say you resell phone cases for $20 each, they cost you $12, and you pay $100 a month for online store fees. Each case contributes $8, and $100 ÷ $8 = 12.5. You can't sell half a case, so always round up: you need 13 cases. At 12 you'd still be $4 short; at 13 you're $4 ahead.

Pricing: what should you charge?

There are two basic ways to think about price, and good prices use both.

  • Cost-plus pricing starts from your costs and adds a markup. If Aisha's full cost at 50 boxes is $420 ÷ 50 = $8.40 a box and she adds a 50% markup, she'd charge $12.60. This makes sure you cover costs, but it ignores what buyers think the product is worth.
  • Value-based pricing starts from what customers are willing to pay. Look at what similar products sell for nearby and what makes yours different: custom designs, local delivery, better ingredients. If people happily pay $15 for a custom box, charging $12.60 leaves money on the table.

Cost sets the floor; what customers will pay sets the ceiling. And one cost is easy to forget: your own time. If you don't count it, you can be busy every weekend and still earn almost nothing for the work.

Common mistake

Underpricing because you forgot your own time. Aisha spends about 20 hours a month baking, decorating and selling. At $15 a box, her $330 profit works out to $16.50 an hour. If she had charged $9 a box to "keep it cheap," 50 boxes would bring in $450 against $420 of costs: a $30 profit, or $1.50 an hour. At that price she'd also need 40 boxes a month just to break even, not 14.

Cash flow is not the same as profit

Profit is a calculation over a period. Cash flow is the actual money moving in and out of your account, and when it moves. A business that is profitable on paper can still run out of cash and be unable to pay its bills. Common reasons:

  • You pay before you get paid. Suppliers, rent and fees are often due right away.
  • Inventory is bought up front. Money spent on stock sitting on a shelf isn't available for anything else.
  • Customers pay late. Some customers, especially other businesses, pay 30 days or more after delivery.

Say a school club orders 40 boxes from Aisha for $600, to be paid 30 days after delivery. She has to spend 40 × $6 = $240 on ingredients and packaging now. On paper the order adds $360 of contribution; in her bank account it's −$240 until the club pays. If she doesn't have that $240, a good order becomes a problem.

Businesses keep track of all this with three standard reports, called financial statements:

  • The income statement (also called a profit and loss statement) shows revenue, expenses and profit over a period, like a month or a year.
  • The balance sheet is a snapshot on one day: what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the difference, which belongs to the owners (equity).
  • The cash flow statement shows the cash that actually came in and went out over a period, which is how you spot a cash squeeze even when profit looks fine.

Key idea

Profit is revenue minus all your costs. Know your break-even point, price for your time and not just your ingredients, and watch cash as closely as profit.

Starting something, and careers in business

You don't need a big idea or a pile of money to start something. You need a real problem, a real customer, and a cheap way to find out if you're right.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Turn a problem people have into a business idea, and test it before spending much money.
  • Write a one-page plan that shows who pays you, what it costs, and where you break even.
  • Describe the main business fields and name ways to explore them while you're in high school.

Start with a problem, not a product

Most new ideas start as "I want to sell this thing." A stronger start is "people I know keep running into this problem." If the problem is real and annoying enough, someone may pay to make it go away. If it isn't, even a great product will sit on the shelf. Look for problems you already understand: kids stuck on homework, neighbors who need a dog walked, classmates with cracked phone screens.

Then get specific about your customer: the person who actually pays. "Everyone" is not a customer. "Parents of middle schoolers in my neighborhood" is. The customer and the user can be different people: a kid gets the tutoring, but a parent pays, so the parent is the one you have to convince.

Test the idea before you spend money

The riskiest move is spending your savings on supplies and a logo, and only then finding out whether anyone wants what you're selling. Test first, in steps that cost little or nothing:

  • Talk to potential customers. Ask about the problem, not your idea: "What's the hardest part of…?" and "What do you do about it now?"
  • Ask for a commitment. A pre-order or small deposit is the strongest signal there is. Plenty of people will say "I'd buy that"; far fewer will hand over money.
  • Start with a small first batch. Bake one tray, not ten. Take three clients, not thirty. This simplest version that customers can actually pay for is called a minimum viable product, or MVP.

Each test either gives you a reason to keep going or saves you from a costly mistake. Both are wins.

Worked example

Sam is good at math and notices younger kids nearby struggling with math homework. Before buying anything, Sam talks to 12 families; 7 say homework help is a real need. Sam offers a one-hour tutoring session for $20, paid in advance, and 4 families pay. That's $80 collected before Sam has spent anything except $8 on flyers.

The first month is the small first batch: those 4 students, once a week for 4 weeks, or 16 sessions. Each session has $4 of variable costs ($1 of printed practice sheets and $3 of bus fare). Fixed costs are the $8 of flyers plus a $40 monthly subscription to a practice-problem website.

A one-page plan, and the practical stuff

You don't need a 40-page business plan. One page that answers five questions is enough to start:

  1. Problem: What problem are you solving?
  2. Customer: Who has it, and who pays?
  3. Solution: What exactly will you offer?
  4. How you make money: What's your price, and how often will people buy?
  5. Costs: What are your fixed and variable costs, and how many sales do you need to break even?

Then check a few practical things. Rules differ from place to place, so treat these as questions to ask, not answers:

  • Age. If you're under 18, you may need a parent or guardian to sign some contracts or open some accounts with you.
  • Local rules. Some businesses need a permit, and selling food made at home often has its own rules. Check with your city, county or state first.
  • Separate money. Use a separate account, or at least a separate record, so you can see whether the business actually makes money.
  • Records for taxes. Write down every dollar in and out. As lesson 2 explained, money you earn working for yourself can be taxable, and no employer is withholding taxes for you. A parent, guardian or tax professional can help you see what applies to you.

Common mistake

Treating "that's a great idea!" as proof. Friends and family are being kind, and saying yes costs them nothing. The real test is whether people who don't owe you a favor will pay or put down a deposit.

Careers in business: the main fields

Almost every organization, from a hospital to a sports team to a nonprofit, needs people who understand money and customers. In college, business is usually split into fields like these:

  • Accounting: recording, checking and reporting an organization's money, including financial statements, audits and taxes. Many accountants become a CPA (Certified Public Accountant). In most states that has generally required 150 semester hours of college, more than a typical four-year degree, plus passing the CPA Exam and work experience. Some states have recently added other routes, so check your state's rules.
  • Finance: how people and companies raise, invest and manage money, from banking to financial planning.
  • Marketing: understanding customers, including research, pricing, advertising and sales.
  • Management: leading people and running operations, from hiring and scheduling to strategy.
  • Entrepreneurship: starting and growing new ventures, including new projects inside larger companies.
  • Business analytics and information systems: using data and technology to make decisions, with spreadsheets, databases, statistics and some programming.

Three skills matter in all of them: using spreadsheets comfortably, communicating clearly in writing and out loud, and working with data to back up a decision. You can start building all three now.

Explore now, and look back at how far you've come

You don't have to pick a career in high school, but you can start finding out what you enjoy:

  • Join a business club like FBLA or DECA if your school has one. They run competitions and events in areas like marketing, finance and entrepreneurship.
  • Try an internship or job shadowing. Even a day watching someone work shows you what a job is really like. Part-time jobs count too: every job teaches you how a business runs.
  • Ask for an informational interview. Ask someone in a field you're curious about for 15 to 20 minutes. Ask what a normal day looks like, what they studied, and what they wish they'd known at your age. Then send a thank-you note.

And look at what you've already learned. You can build a budget and read a paycheck. You know how saving and investing let money grow over time, and how credit can help you or cost you. You know how a business makes money and when it breaks even, and now how to start something of your own. That's a real foundation, and you're building it years earlier than most people do.

Key idea

Start from a real problem and a real customer, test small before you spend, and know your numbers. You don't need your future figured out: keep trying things and let what you learn guide the next step.

Banking basics

Almost every paycheck you'll ever get will land in a bank account, so it pays to know how accounts work and how to stop fees from quietly eating your money.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain why a bank or credit union is safer than cash, and what deposit insurance covers.
  • Use checking, savings, a debit card and ATMs without paying fees you don't need to.
  • Explain how an overdraft happens, and check a bank statement for mistakes.

Why use a bank or credit union at all?

Cash in a drawer can be lost, stolen or burned, and once it's gone, it's gone. Money in an account is safer for a few reasons:

  • Deposit insurance. Banks insured by the FDIC and credit unions insured by the NCUA protect your deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category. If the bank or credit union fails, you get your insured money back. You can check an institution at FDIC.gov or NCUA.gov. Some money apps aren't banks, so check how yours protects your money.
  • A record. Every deposit and payment is written down, so you can track your money and prove you paid someone. A good banking history also helps later, when you rent or borrow.
  • No check-cashing fees. Without an account, cashing a paycheck often means paying a check-cashing store. At an assumed fee of 3%, cashing a $400 paycheck costs $12. Do that twice a month and it's $24 a month, or $288 a year, just to get your own money.

Banks are businesses owned by shareholders and run to make a profit. Credit unions are nonprofits owned by their members; you usually join based on where you live, work or go to school, or through family. Credit unions often have lower fees and big banks often have more branches, but neither is always better. Compare the actual accounts.

Checking, savings and your debit card

Most people have two accounts that work together:

  • A checking account is for everyday money in and out: your paycheck arrives here, and your card purchases and bills come out of it.
  • A savings account is for money you're not spending yet, like an emergency fund or a car goal. It usually pays some interest and is a little harder to spend on impulse, which is the point.

A debit card takes money straight out of your checking account. It is not a credit card: you're spending your own money, not borrowing. (The Money & Business Foundations course covers credit.) At an ATM or some store checkouts, you'll type your PIN, a short secret number. Never share it, never write it on the card, and cover the keypad when you type it.

Your bank's own ATMs, and those in its network, are usually free. An out-of-network ATM often charges a fee, and your own bank may add a second fee on top. Using an in-network ATM, or getting cash back when you pay at a store, usually avoids both.

Common fees, and how to avoid them

  • Monthly maintenance fee: a charge just for having the account. Many student accounts skip it, and others waive it if you set up direct deposit or keep a minimum balance.
  • Minimum balance fee: charged if your balance falls below a set amount. Know the number before you open the account.
  • Overdraft fee: an overdraft happens when you spend more than is in your checking account and the bank pays anyway, leaving you with a negative balance. Then it charges you a fee, which can be far bigger than the purchase.
  • NSF fee: if the bank refuses a payment instead, such as a check or automatic bill, it may charge a nonsufficient funds (NSF) fee, also called a returned-item fee. The bill still isn't paid, and the company may add a late fee.

For ATM withdrawals and everyday debit card purchases, federal rules say a bank can charge an overdraft fee only if you've opted in to overdraft coverage. If you haven't, the card is simply declined. Fees vary, and some banks charge little or nothing, so read your account's fee list. Low-balance alerts and linking savings to cover overdrafts can also help.

Common mistake

Assuming your debit card will just be declined if you don't have enough money. If you've opted in to overdraft coverage, the purchase goes through and the fee follows. Know your real balance, including payments that are still pending, before you tap your card.

Your statement and your banking app

Your bank's app makes day-to-day banking easier:

Smart spending: getting your money's worth

The price on the tag is rarely the whole story, and stores are very good at making you forget that.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Compare products by unit price and work out what a sale or coupon really saves.
  • Spot the costs that come later: sales tax, pay-later plans, subscriptions and upkeep.
  • Recognize common marketing pressure and slow down before an impulse buy.

Compare the price per unit, not per package

Two bottles of shampoo sit side by side: one is bigger and costs more. Which is the better deal? You can't tell from the price alone. You need the unit price: the price divided by the amount you get, such as dollars per ounce or cents per sheet.

Many stores print the unit price in small type on the shelf tag. If they don't, divide it yourself: price ÷ size. Make sure both products use the same unit (ounces with ounces, not ounces with "count").

Bigger is often cheaper per unit, but not always. A smaller size may be on sale, or a store may simply price the big one higher. And a bulk bargain only saves money if you'll use it all before it goes bad. Try the calculator below with any two sizes you find.

Sales, coupons and stacked discounts

Percent off is simple once you turn it into money. For 30% off a $45 hoodie, 30% of $45 is $13.50, so you pay $31.50. A shortcut: 30% off means you pay 70%, and 70% of $45 is $31.50.

"Buy one, get one" deals depend on the fine print. "Buy one, get one free" on a $6 item means two for $6, or $3 each: 50% off, but only if you actually wanted two. "Buy one, get one 50% off" on a $12 item means $18 for two, or $9 each. That's only 25% off per item.

Stacked discounts don't simply add up. If a jacket is 20% off and you get "an extra 10% off at the register", the second discount comes off the already-reduced price. On a $50 jacket: 20% off leaves $40, then 10% off $40 leaves $36. That's $14 off in total, or 28% off, not 30%.

At the register and after: tax, receipts and warranties

In the US, the price on the tag usually doesn't include sales tax; it's added when you pay. The rate depends on your state and often your city or county too, and a handful of states have no statewide sales tax at all. Some items, like groceries, may be taxed at a lower rate or not at all, depending on where you live.

Keep your receipt, or a photo of it, until you're sure you're keeping the item. Check the return policy before you buy: many stores give you a set number of days, some charge a restocking fee, and "final sale" items often can't be returned at all.

Many products come with a manufacturer's warranty that covers defects for a set time. At checkout you may be offered an extended warranty for extra money. Read what it covers and compare its price with the cost of simply replacing the item. For cheap items, an extended warranty is often not worth it.

Costs that keep coming

Buy now, pay later plans split a purchase into a few payments, often four over several weeks. Some charge no interest if every payment is on time, but missed payments can bring late fees, and several plans at once are easy to lose track of. Store financing and store credit cards work in a similar way over longer periods. Watch for deferred interest offers ("no interest if paid in full in 12 months"): if any balance is left when the promotion ends, interest can be charged all the way back to the purchase date. Paying later doesn't make anything cheaper; at best it costs the same. The Money & Business Foundations course covers interest and credit in more depth.

Subscriptions and free trials usually auto-renew: when the trial ends, your card is charged unless you cancel. A forgotten $9.99-a-month trial costs $119.88 a year. When you start a trial, set a reminder to decide a day or two before it ends.

Finally, think about total cost of ownership: the price to buy something plus what it costs to use it. A cheap printer can need expensive ink. With example numbers, a $40 printer that needs a $30 cartridge every two months costs $40 + (12 × $30) = $400 over two years. Game consoles, phones and cars work the same way: add up the extras, not just the sticker.

Marketing tricks and the 24-hour rule

Stores and brands want you to buy now. A few common tactics:

  • Urgency: "Only 3 left!", countdown timers, "today only". Pressure to decide fast leaves no time to think.
  • Anchoring: "Was $120, now $60!" The "was" price sets a reference point that makes $60 feel like a win, even if the item rarely sold for $120. Ask whether it's worth $60 to you.
  • Influencer ads: a creator you like shows off a product. They may be paid or given it free. Paid posts are supposed to be labeled, with tags like #ad or "paid partnership", but they're still ads.
  • Free shipping thresholds: "Free shipping over $35" can nudge a $28 cart up to $35 with things you didn't plan to buy.

A simple defense is the 24-hour rule: for anything that isn't a planned or needed purchase, wait a day before buying. If you still want it tomorrow and it fits your plan, buy it. Often the urge has passed.

Key idea

Compare unit prices, turn every discount into dollars, add the tax and the costs that come later, and give yourself time before you buy.

Protecting your money: scams and identity theft

Scammers don't need to hack anything if they can get you to hand over the money, or the code, yourself.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Recognize the warning signs that most scams share, and the scams teens run into most.
  • Protect your accounts with strong passwords, two-factor authentication and codes you never share.
  • Spot the signs of identity theft and know exactly where to report it.

The warning signs most scams share

Most scams use the same few tricks. Learn them and you can spot scams you've never seen before:

  • Urgency. "Act in the next 30 minutes or your account will be closed." Pressure stops you from thinking or asking someone.
  • Secrecy. "Don't tell your parents" or "keep this between us." Real businesses and friends don't need you to hide things.
  • Unusual ways to pay. Gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency or a payment app. These are fast and very hard to reverse. Anyone who demands to be paid in gift cards is a scammer, every time.
  • Too good to be true. A prize you never entered for, or $500 a week for "a few minutes a day."

If a message hits even one of these, slow down. Check with someone you trust, or contact the company using a website or number you already know, not the one in the message.

Scams teens actually run into

  • Phishing texts and emails. A message that seems to be from your bank, a delivery company or your school, with a link to "confirm your account" on a fake login page. Open the real app or website yourself instead.
  • Fake online stores. Slick sites or social media ads with prices far below anywhere else. You pay, and nothing arrives, or a cheap fake does.
  • Marketplace overpayment scams. A buyer "accidentally" pays too much and asks for the difference back (see the worked example).
  • Fake jobs, "reshipping" and money-mule offers. "Jobs" that have you reship packages or pass money through your account. The goods and money are usually stolen, and moving money for someone else can be a crime, even if you didn't know.
  • Fake prizes and scholarship scams. "You've won! Just pay a small fee to claim it." Real prizes don't charge you to collect, and you never pay to get a scholarship.
  • Romance and friendship scams. Someone you've only met online becomes close over weeks, then suddenly has an emergency and needs money or gift cards.
  • "Verification code" scams. "Hey, I accidentally sent my code to your number, can you send it to me?" That code is the key to your account. Send it, and they can lock you out and scam your friends as you.

Worked example

Kenji lists his old gaming headset on an online marketplace for $150. A buyer quickly sends $400 through a payment app. "Oops, wrong amount! Can you send back the extra $250? I'll pick up the headset tomorrow."

It seems fair: $400 − $150 = $250. Kenji sends $250 back and hands over the headset. A few days later, the $400 vanishes. The buyer paid with a stolen card or hacked account, and when the real owner reported the fraud, that payment was reversed. Kenji's $250 was a normal payment he chose to send, and it's gone.

Payment apps work like cash

Peer-to-peer payment apps are great for splitting a pizza. But sending money with one usually works like handing over cash: it moves in seconds, and if you paid a scammer, the app often won't refund it because you approved the payment.

Money coming to you is different. If it came from a stolen card or account, it can be pulled back later, as Kenji found out. Fake checks can bounce the same way. So send app payments only to people you know in real life, double-check the name before you tap send, and don't treat a stranger's payment as final.

Lock down your accounts

Many account takeovers come from reused passwords or shared codes, not movie-style hacking. Four habits help:

  • Use a strong, unique password for every account. Long beats clever: a passphrase of four or more random words, like "copper-lantern-mango-sleet", is easier to remember and harder to guess than "P@ssw0rd1". Unique means a leak at one site puts only that account at risk.
  • Consider a password manager. This is an app or browser feature that creates and remembers a strong password for each account, so you remember just one master passphrase.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA). The site also asks for a second proof, usually a one-time code sent to your phone or shown in an authenticator app, so a stolen password alone isn't enough.
  • Never share a one-time code. Not with a "friend," "customer support" or someone who says they sent it by mistake. Real companies won't ask you to read one back.

Common mistake

Trusting a message because it comes from a friend's account. A scammer who takes over an account messages all its contacts, and the requests feel safe because the name is familiar. If a friend suddenly asks for a code, money or gift cards, call them or ask in person first.

Identity theft: what it is and what to do

Identity theft is when someone uses your personal information, such as your name, birthday or Social Security number, to open accounts, borrow money, or file taxes as you. They get the money; you get the bills and damaged credit (the Money & Business Foundations course covers credit scores).

Teens are a favorite target because you usually have a clean record, often no credit file at all, so a thief can build one in your name. It can go unnoticed for years, until you apply for a student loan, car loan or apartment and get turned down.

Warning signs include bills or collection calls for accounts you never opened, charges you don't recognize, being turned down because of a credit history you don't have, or a notice that a tax return was already filed in your name. If it happens, move quickly:

  • Contact your bank or the company involved (look up the number yourself) and change your passwords.
  • Report identity theft at IdentityTheft.gov, which creates a personal recovery plan.
  • Report scams and fraud at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, even if you didn't lose money.
  • Freeze your credit. It stops lenders from seeing your credit report, so it's very hard for anyone to open new credit in your name. It's free by federal law, and a parent or guardian can request one for a child under 16.

Adults can check their credit reports free at AnnualCreditReport.com. For a minor, a parent or guardian can ask each credit bureau whether a report even exists.

Key idea

Urgency, secrecy and strange payment methods mean stop and check. Keep your passwords unique and your codes private, treat payment-app money like cash, and report fraud fast.

Insurance: paying a little to avoid losing a lot

Insurance is a deal: you pay a small, known cost every month so that one bad day can't wipe out years of savings.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain how insurance works and read the words on a policy: premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance and more.
  • Compare a low-premium plan with a low-deductible plan for a given year's bills.
  • Tell apart the main kinds of auto, health and renters coverage, and spot when insurance isn't worth buying.

What insurance actually does

Some losses are small: a lost water bottle. Others are huge and rare: a car crash, a hospital stay, an apartment fire. You can't know in advance whether one will happen to you this year, but across a big group of people, it's fairly predictable how many will happen to someone.

That's the idea behind insurance: pooling risk. Imagine 100 students who each own a $1,000 laptop, and in a typical year about two of those laptops get destroyed. If everyone pays $20 into a shared pot, the pot holds $2,000, which is enough to replace both. Each student trades a small, certain cost ($20) for protection against a large, uncertain one ($1,000).

Real insurers work the same way, but they also cover their costs and profit, so on average people pay in more than they get back. That's fine: you're not buying insurance to come out ahead. You're buying it so that a disaster you can't afford becomes a cost you can.

The words on every policy

The same words show up whether you're insuring a car, your health or an apartment.

  • Your policy is the contract: what's covered, what isn't, and what it costs.
  • The premium is what you pay to keep the policy active, usually every month. You pay it whether or not anything goes wrong.
  • When something does go wrong, you file a claim: a request for the insurer to pay.
  • The deductible is how much of the covered costs you pay yourself before the insurer starts paying.
  • Health plans often add a copay (a flat fee, like a set amount per doctor visit) and coinsurance (a percentage you share after the deductible). With 20% coinsurance, a $1,000 covered bill means you pay $200 and the plan pays $800.
  • The out-of-pocket maximum is the most you'll pay for covered care in a year; after that, the plan pays 100% of covered costs. Your premiums don't count toward it.
  • A coverage limit is the most the insurer will pay. Anything above it is on you.

Low premium or low deductible?

Most insurance lets you choose between two kinds of plans, and they pull in opposite directions:

  • Low premium, high deductible: cheaper every month, but you pay more yourself when something happens.
  • High premium, low deductible: more expensive every month, but less of a hit when something happens.

Neither is always better. If you rarely need the coverage, the low premium usually wins. If you expect big bills, the low deductible usually wins. And whichever plan you pick, you need to be able to pay its deductible if the worst happens. Try the calculator below to compare two plans for any amount of bills.

Picking the lowest premium without asking "could I pay the deductible tomorrow?" A plan that saves you $100 a month doesn't help much if a $2,500 bill arrives and you have $200 in the bank. A high deductible works best when you have an emergency fund that could cover it.

Auto insurance

Almost every state requires drivers to carry liability coverage. It pays for damage and injuries you cause to other people and their property, up to your policy's coverage limits. It does not pay to fix your own car. That's what the other two main types are for:

  • Collision pays to repair your car after a crash, whether you hit another car or a pole.
  • Comprehensive covers damage that isn't a crash: theft, hail, fire, vandalism or hitting a deer.

Collision and comprehensive are usually optional, though a lender typically requires them if you have a car loan. Lesson 6 looks at buying a car.

Teen drivers usually pay more than almost anyone else, because insurers see new drivers as more likely to crash. Premiums can come down in a few general ways: many insurers offer good-student discounts, discounts for finishing a driver's education course, and lower rates for a clean driving record. Staying on a family policy, driving a less expensive car and choosing a higher deductible can also help.

Health, renters and phone plans

Health insurance. Young adults can generally stay on a parent's health plan until age 26, even if they move out or aren't in school. Most plans have a network of doctors and hospitals that have agreed on prices with the plan. Staying in-network costs you less; going out-of-network can cost much more or may not be covered at all. Check before you book an appointment.

Renters insurance. When you rent, your landlord's insurance covers the building, not your stuff. Renters insurance covers your belongings (clothes, laptop, furniture) if they're stolen or damaged by something like a fire, and it usually includes liability coverage if someone is hurt in your home. It's usually inexpensive compared with what it would cost to replace everything you own.

Phone and device protection plans. Stores and carriers often offer a plan for a monthly fee, and many also charge a deductible each time you file a claim. Before signing up, add it up: a plan at an example price of $10 a month costs $120 a year and $240 over two years, before any deductible. Compare that with what a repair or replacement would actually cost you.

When insurance isn't worth it. Insurance makes the most sense for losses you couldn't cover yourself. For small, affordable losses, like cheap earbuds or a phone case, you'll usually do better by skipping the coverage and keeping that money in savings to pay for the rare replacement.

Key idea

Insure the losses you couldn't afford, not the ones you could. When choosing a plan, look at the total yearly cost, premiums plus what you'd pay out of pocket, not just the monthly price.

Paying for college

The price on a college's website is rarely what you'll pay, and the biggest aid offer isn't always the best deal.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Tell the difference between cost of attendance, sticker price and net price.
  • Explain what the FAFSA does and which kinds of aid have to be repaid.
  • Compare two aid offers by net price and by how much of each is loans.

What college really costs

A year of college costs more than tuition. The cost of attendance is a college's estimate of everything a year will take:

  • Tuition and fees: what the college charges for classes and services.
  • Housing and food: a dorm and meal plan, or rent and groceries.
  • Books and supplies: textbooks, a laptop, lab materials.
  • Transportation: getting to campus and home for breaks.
  • Personal expenses: a phone bill, toiletries, laundry, the occasional meal out.

The sticker price is the full, published price before any help. Many students don't pay it. What matters is the net price: the cost of attendance minus the grants and scholarships you receive, which is money you don't pay back.

That means a college with a scary sticker price can end up cheaper than one that looks affordable. To get an early estimate, look for the net price calculator on each college's website. You answer questions about your family's finances and it estimates what you might actually pay there. It's only an estimate, but it's far more useful than the sticker price.

The FAFSA

The FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the form that opens the door to federal grants, work-study and federal student loans. Many colleges and states also use your FAFSA to decide their own aid, so skipping it can cost you money from several places at once.

A few things to know:

  • You file it every year you want aid, not just once.
  • It asks about your family's income, so you'll usually fill it out with a parent or guardian.
  • It's free. The official site is StudentAid.gov, and that's also where to check the current opening date and deadlines. Colleges and states can have their own, earlier deadlines, so check those too.

Even if you think your family earns too much to qualify, filing can still matter: some colleges won't consider you for their own aid without it.

The four kinds of aid

When a college accepts you, it sends an aid offer listing the help it can give. Everything on it falls into one of four groups:

  • Grants are usually based on financial need and come from the federal government, your state or the college. You generally don't repay them.
  • Scholarships are often based on grades, talents, activities or background, and come from colleges and outside groups. You generally don't repay them either.
  • Work-study is a chance to earn money at a part-time job, often on campus. You earn it as you work, so it isn't taken off your bill up front.
  • Loans are borrowed money. You repay them, with interest.

Only grants and scholarships lower your net price. Work-study is a paycheck, and loans are a bill you'll pay later. That's why comparing offers takes more than looking at the biggest total: an aid offer full of loans isn't free money.

Student loans: federal vs. private

If you borrow, the kind of loan matters as much as the amount.

  • Federal student loans come from the US government through the FAFSA. They have fixed interest rates, so your rate won't change over the life of the loan. They also come with protections, including income-driven repayment options that can lower your monthly payment if your income is low.
  • Private student loans come from banks, credit unions and other lenders. Students often need a co-signer, usually a parent, who is equally responsible for the debt. Rates can be fixed or variable, and they generally offer fewer protections if you have trouble paying.

Because of those differences, many families look at federal loans first and private loans only to fill a remaining gap. Either way, a loan is a promise to make monthly payments for years. Common mistake

Picking the college with the biggest "total aid" number. Aid offers often add grants, work-study and loans into one figure, and they don't all look the same from college to college. Pull out the grants and scholarships, work out the net price yourself, and then check how much of what's left is loans.

How much to borrow, and cheaper paths

A common guideline is to try to keep your total student debt below what you expect to earn in your first year after graduating. It's a rule of thumb, not a law, and pay varies a lot by career, but it's a useful check. If Andre expects to start at around $45,000 a year (an example figure), College A's $16,000 is well under that, while College B's $48,000 is over it.

There are also ways to lower the cost of a degree:

  • Community college, then transfer. Taking your first one or two years at a community college usually costs much less. Check early that your credits will transfer to the four-year school you want.
  • In-state public colleges. Public colleges usually charge students from their own state less than students from other states.
  • Earning credit in high school. Dual enrollment classes and AP exams can count toward a degree, depending on each college's rules, which means fewer courses to pay for later.

Key idea

Judge a college by its net price, not its sticker price, and read every aid offer for how much of it you'll have to pay back.

Big purchases: your first car and your first apartment

The sticker price and the monthly rent are only the start. The real cost of a car or an apartment is everything that comes with it.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Add up the total monthly cost of owning a car, not just the payment.
  • Compare car loans and explain why a longer loan costs more in total.
  • Estimate what you can afford in rent, read the key parts of a lease and protect your deposit.

What a car really costs

When people talk about a car's cost, they usually mean the price or the monthly payment. The total cost of ownership is bigger. Every month or year you'll also pay for:

  • Insurance. Almost every state requires auto liability insurance, and a lender usually requires more coverage. Lesson 4 explains how premiums and deductibles work.
  • Gas (or charging), which depends on how far you drive.
  • Maintenance and repairs: oil changes, tires, brakes and surprise repairs.
  • Registration and fees your state charges to keep the car on the road, often yearly.

Here's an example with made-up numbers: a $300 payment, $150 insurance, $120 gas, $50 set aside for maintenance, and $20 for registration (a $240 yearly fee spread over 12 months). That car costs $640 a month, more than twice the payment.

New or used? New cars generally lose value fastest in their first few years, so the first owner absorbs the biggest drop. A used car lets someone else take that hit, but it may need more repairs and has less warranty left. Compare the total cost of the specific cars you're considering.

If you buy used, a few checks protect you:

  • Get a vehicle history report using the car's VIN (vehicle identification number) to look for past accidents, flood damage or a salvage title.
  • Pay for an independent inspection by a mechanic you choose, not one the seller picks.
  • Test drive it, check for open safety recalls, and make sure the seller's name and the car's VIN match the title.

Shop for the loan before the car

Many people borrow to buy a car, and it pays to find the loan first. You can apply to a bank or credit union for preapproval: the lender reviews your finances and tells you how much it will lend and at what rate. Then you walk into the dealership knowing your budget and holding a rate to beat. If the dealer offers financing, compare its APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of borrowing including interest) with yours, and pick whichever costs less.

The other big choice is the loan term, how many months you take to pay. A longer term spreads the same amount over more payments, so each payment is smaller. But you're borrowing the money for longer, so you pay interest for longer, and the total you pay goes up. A bigger down payment (cash you pay up front) shrinks the loan and the interest.

Your first apartment: rent and move-in costs

A common guideline is to keep rent at or below about 30% of your gross income (what you earn before taxes). It's a rule of thumb, not a law. If you earn $3,000 a month before taxes, 30% is $900.

Moving in costs much more than one month's rent. Expect:

  • First month's rent, and sometimes the last month's too.
  • A security deposit, often around one month's rent, held by the landlord to cover unpaid rent or damage beyond normal wear.
  • Application fees for background and credit checks, usually per adult applicant.
  • Utility setup: electricity, gas, internet, and sometimes a deposit if you have no credit history.

For a $1,000 apartment with a $1,000 deposit, a $40 application fee and $100 in utility setup, you'd need $2,140 before you sleep there once, before furniture or moving costs.

The lease, roommates and your deposit

A lease is a legal contract. Read all of it before you sign, and ask about anything unclear. Look especially for:

  • The term: how long it lasts (often 12 months) and what happens at the end.
  • The deposit: what it can be used for and how and when it's returned. Rules vary by state.
  • Who fixes what: which repairs are the landlord's job, and how to request them.
  • Breaking the lease: if you leave early, you may owe a fee or rent until the unit is re-rented.
  • Guests and pets: limits on overnight guests, pet rules, and any pet fees or deposits.

Roommates make rent cheaper; $1,800 split two ways is $900 each. But if you both sign the lease, the landlord can often hold either of you responsible for the full rent if the other stops paying. Agree in writing on who pays what (rent, utilities, internet, shared supplies), when, and what happens if someone moves out.

Your landlord's insurance usually covers the building, not your belongings. Renters insurance covers your stuff and your liability, often for a modest monthly premium; see Lesson 4 for how policies work.

Finally, document the unit's condition on move-in day. Take dated photos and videos of every room and any scratches, stains or damage, note them on the move-in checklist if there is one, and keep a copy. When you move out, that record helps show which damage was already there, so it doesn't come out of your deposit.

Key idea

Judge a big purchase by its total cost, not its monthly number: add every cost that comes with it, compare loans by total interest, and read and document everything before you sign.

Finding work that fits you

Your first job doesn't have to be your dream job, but it can fit who you are and the hours you actually have.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Name your skills and interests and connect them to kinds of work.
  • Tell apart part-time, seasonal, volunteer, internship and apprenticeship roles.
  • Know where to look for openings, and spot a job offer that's fake.

Start with you: skills and interests

Before you look at a single job posting, look at yourself. A skill is something you can do. An interest is something you like doing or want to learn more about. The best first jobs use a bit of both.

Some skills are specific, like using a cash register, speaking Spanish or editing video. Others are transferable skills that help in almost any job: showing up on time, being friendly with strangers, staying calm when it's busy, following directions, working on a team.

You probably have more of these than you think. If you've kept a younger sibling out of trouble, you've shown responsibility. If you've played on a team, you've shown teamwork and commitment. If people ask you to fix their phone settings, you're good with technology.

Kinds of first jobs

"A job" can mean several different things. The main kinds open to students are:

  • Part-time jobs: a few shifts a week, often evenings and weekends, at places like restaurants, stores, grocery stores, movie theaters or recreation centers.
  • Seasonal jobs: work that only exists for part of the year, like summer camp counselor, lifeguard, holiday retail help or yard work in the fall.
  • Volunteering: unpaid work for a cause, like a food bank, library or animal shelter. You don't earn money, but you build skills, experience and people who can vouch for you.
  • Internships: short-term positions where you learn how a field works, such as an office, a hospital or a local government. Some are paid and some aren't.
  • Apprenticeships: paid jobs that train you in a skilled trade, like electrical work, plumbing or advanced manufacturing, by combining work with classroom learning. Some programs are open to high-school students; many start after graduation.

You don't have to pick one forever. Many students volunteer at 14, take a part-time job at 16 and try an internship or apprenticeship later.

Your time, and the rules for teen workers

A job has to fit around school, sports, family and sleep. Before you apply, write out a normal week and count the hours you are truly free. Employers will ask for your availability, and it's far better to offer fewer hours you can keep than more hours you can't.

There are also rules. Federal law (the Fair Labor Standards Act) sets limits on the hours and kinds of work for workers under 18, and many states add their own rules, such as requiring a work permit. Rules for teen workers vary by state; check your state's labor department. The US Department of Labor (DOL.gov) also explains the federal rules.

Where to look

Openings show up in more places than one website:

  • People you know: family, neighbors, coaches, teachers and friends with jobs often hear about openings first. Tell them what you're looking for.
  • Your school: counselors and career centers often know local employers who hire students.
  • In person: "Now hiring" signs are real. Walk in at a quiet time, ask for the manager and ask how to apply.
  • Online: job boards, the careers page on an employer's own website, and your city's parks and recreation department.

Apply to several places, not just one. Hearing "no" or nothing at all is normal, and it isn't about you.

Common mistake

Only looking online and then waiting. Plenty of first jobs are filled by people who asked in person or heard about them from someone they know. Use every channel, and follow up.

Spotting fake job offers

Some "jobs" are scams aimed at people eager for work. Warning signs include:

  • You're asked to pay for training, a starter kit or equipment before you begin.
  • The pay sounds far too high for very little work, or you're "hired" without any interview.
  • You're asked for bank details or your Social Security number before you've met anyone or received a real offer.
  • You're sent a check and told to deposit it and send part of the money somewhere else.

After you're hired, a real employer will need your Social Security number and ID for tax and work forms. Before that, be careful. The Financial Literacy course's lesson on scams goes deeper; if something feels off, stop and ask a parent or trusted adult.

Key idea

Start from your skills, interests and the hours you really have, look in several places, and walk away from any "job" that wants money or bank details from you first.

Resumes and applications

You have more experience than you think; a resume is just a clear, honest way to show it on one page.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Build a one-page first resume, even with little work experience.
  • Write a short cover message and choose your references.
  • Fill in a job application completely and honestly.

What a resume is for

A resume is a one-page summary of who you are, what you've done and how to reach you. Managers often read it quickly, so the goal is to make the most useful facts easy to find, not to fill space.

A first resume usually has these parts, in this order:

  • Contact information: your name, phone number, a simple email address (your name, not a nickname or joke) and your city and state. You don't need your full street address.
  • Education: your school, expected graduation year, and anything worth noting, like honors classes or a strong GPA if you want to share it.
  • Experience: paid jobs, volunteering and informal work, most recent first.
  • Skills: things like languages, computer programs, certifications such as first aid or CPR.
  • Activities and awards: clubs, sports, music, leadership roles.

Leave off your Social Security number, a photo, your age, and personal details like religion. They don't belong on a resume.

Experience that counts

If you've never had a paycheck, you still have experience. Employers hiring teens know you're just starting and are looking for signs you're reliable, responsible and willing to learn. All of these count:

  • School: group projects, tutoring classmates, being a team captain or club officer.
  • Volunteering: a food bank, a place of worship, a school event, a community cleanup.
  • Informal work: babysitting, pet sitting, mowing lawns, shoveling snow, helping at a family business.

Put these under a heading like "Experience" or "Work and volunteer experience". What you did matters more than whether you were paid for it.

Writing strong resume lines

Under each experience, write two or three short bullet points. Good bullets:

  • Start with an action verb: organized, helped, led, sold, cleaned, trained, scheduled.
  • Say what you actually did, with specific, true details and numbers where they fit: how many kids, how many hours, how often.
  • Skip "I" and full sentences. Keep each bullet to one line if you can.

Then proofread. Read it aloud, check every date and phone number, and ask an adult to look it over. A typo in your phone number means no one can call you back.

Common mistake

Stretching the truth. Calling yourself "fluent" in a language you studied for one year, or listing a job you didn't have, can be discovered in one conversation or one phone call. Employers can let someone go later for lying on an application. Describe real experience well instead of inventing experience.

A simple cover message

A cover message (a cover letter, when it's longer and more formal) is a short note that goes with your resume, as an email, in an application's message box, or on paper. For a first job, a few sentences in three parts is enough:

  • Which job, and how you heard about it.
  • Why you'd be good at it, with one or two real examples.
  • Your availability and a thank-you.

For example: "Hi, I'm applying for the weekend bagger position on the sign in your front window. I've handled cash at our booster club snack table and walked neighbors' dogs on a schedule for two summers, so I'm used to being on time and working with people. I'm available Saturdays and Sundays. Thank you for considering me. Jordan." In an email, use a clear subject line, like "Application: weekend bagger, Jordan".

References and applications

A reference is an adult who can tell an employer what you're like: a teacher, coach, volunteer coordinator, club advisor or a neighbor you've worked for. Family members usually aren't good references. Aim for two or three, and always ask first. Give them a copy of your resume and tell them what jobs you're applying for, so they aren't surprised by a call.

A job application is the employer's own form, online or on paper. Fill in every field; write "N/A" (not applicable) where something doesn't fit you, instead of leaving it blank. Be accurate about your availability, dates and contact details, and answer every question honestly. Keep a single sheet with your references' contact information and your experience dates, so each application takes minutes, not an hour.

Key idea

Everything you've done can count if you describe it clearly and honestly: one page, action verbs, real details, and references who've agreed to vouch for you.

Interviews

An interview is a conversation, not a test, and most of how it goes is decided before you walk in the door.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Prepare for an interview and practice answers to common questions.
  • Answer "tell me about a time" questions with a short situation-action-result story.
  • Ask good questions of your own and follow up with a thank-you.

Before the interview

An employer who asks you to interview already thinks you might be a fit. Now you get to show them. Preparing turns nerves into confidence:

  • Learn about the place. Re-read the job posting. Know what the business does and what the job involves. If it's a store or restaurant, visit as a customer first.
  • Plan how you'll get there. Work out the route and travel time, and aim to arrive about 10 minutes early. For a video interview, test your camera, sound and internet the day before, and pick a quiet spot.
  • Bring the basics. A couple of copies of your resume, your list of references, a pen, and your availability written down.
  • Dress one step neater than what employees wear on the job. Clean and tidy matters more than expensive.

Most of all, practice out loud. Ask a parent, friend or teacher to play the interviewer. Answers that sound fine in your head often come out rambling the first time you say them.

Common questions

Many interviews for first jobs use some version of these:

  • "Tell me about yourself." Not your life story: your grade and school, one or two strengths, and why you want this job. About a minute.
  • "Why do you want to work here?" Use what you learned: "I shop here and the staff are always helpful; I'd like to be part of that."
  • "What's your availability?" Give the real days and hours you can work. Don't promise hours you can't keep.
  • "What's a strength? A weakness?" For a weakness, choose something real but not alarming, and say what you're doing about it.
  • "Tell me about a time you..." worked on a team, solved a problem, made a mistake. These are behavioral questions, and the next section shows how to answer them.

If you don't know an answer, it's fine to pause, or to say "I haven't done that yet, but here's how I'd learn." Interviewers generally shouldn't ask about things like your religion, race or health. If a question makes you uncomfortable, you can steer back to the job, and tell a parent or trusted adult afterward.

Answer with a short story

"I'm a hard worker" is easy to say and hard to believe. A short true story is proof. Build it in three parts:

  • Situation: where you were and what the problem or task was. One or two sentences.
  • Action: what you did. This is the biggest part. Say "I", not "we", so they know your role.
  • Result: how it turned out, with a number if you have one. If it didn't go perfectly, say what you learned.

Your stories can come from anywhere: school, sports, volunteering, babysitting, a family business. Prepare three or four, and you can adapt them to most "tell me about a time" questions.

Worked example

Sofia, 17, has an interview at 4:00 p.m. for a cashier job at a neighborhood grocery store. She wants to arrive by 3:50. The bus ride takes 25 minutes and the walk from the stop takes 5, so she must leave by 3:20. She plans to leave at 3:15 in case the bus is late.

She expects "Tell me about a time you solved a problem," so she prepares this story:

PartWhat Sofia says
Situation"I was running the cash box at our club's bake sale, and halfway through, the card reader stopped working while a line was forming."

Result"The line kept moving, the reader worked again after about 15 minutes, and we raised $240, a little over our $200 goal."

She practices it out loud with a timer until it takes under a minute. It shows exactly what a cashier needs: handling money, staying calm with a line of customers, and solving problems.

Questions to ask them

Near the end, you'll usually hear "Do you have any questions for us?" Have two or three ready. Good ones show you're thinking about doing the job well:

  • "What does a typical shift look like?"
  • "What kind of training do new employees get?"
  • "What makes someone really good at this job?"
  • "What are the next steps, and when should I expect to hear back?"

It's reasonable to ask about pay and hours, especially once they bring it up or offer you the job. Lesson 5 shows how to compare offers.

Common mistake

Saying "No, I think you covered everything" when asked for questions. It can sound like you aren't very interested. Even one thoughtful question, like asking about training, leaves a better last impression.

After the interview: follow up

Send a thank-you note within a day. An email is fine. Keep it short: thank them for their time, mention one specific thing you talked about, and say you're still interested. For example: "Thank you for meeting with me today. I enjoyed hearing about how new cashiers train alongside experienced staff. I'm excited about the job and hope to hear from you. Sofia."

If they said they'd decide within a week and you haven't heard back, one polite follow-up message is fine. If you don't get this job, ask yourself what went well and what you'd change, and keep applying. Every interview is practice for the next one.

Key idea

Prepare and practice out loud, prove your strengths with short situation-action-result stories, ask a question or two of your own, and send a thank-you within a day.

Your first weeks on the job

You got the job. The first few weeks are when your manager decides whether you're someone they can count on.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Build a reputation for reliability from your very first shift.
  • Write clear, professional texts and emails to a manager, and take feedback well.
  • Handle a mistake the right way and know your basic rights at work.

What the first weeks are really like

Most new jobs start with onboarding: paperwork, a tour, safety rules, and training with a coworker or manager. You'll probably feel slow and a bit lost. That's normal. Nobody expects you to know everything in week one.

What people do notice is how you learn. Write things down so you don't have to ask the same question five times. Learn your coworkers' names. If there's an employee handbook, read it: it explains the rules on things like breaks, dress code, phones and how to call out sick.

Ask questions, especially about anything involving safety, money or customers. "Can you show me that one more time?" is a sign that you care about doing it right, not a sign that you're bad at the job.

Reliability: the skill that matters most

At a first job, being dependable often matters more than being fast or talented. A manager can teach you to run a register. They can't teach you to show up.

  • Know your schedule. Put every shift in your phone's calendar with a reminder the moment it's posted.
  • Arrive a few minutes early, ready to work: in uniform, phone away, done with your snack.
  • If you'll be late or you're sick, tell your manager as early as you can, using whatever method your workplace requires. Some want a phone call, not a text. Texting a coworker doesn't count.
  • Never skip a shift without telling anyone. A no-call, no-show is one of the fastest ways to lose a job.
  • Ask for time off early, and follow the workplace's process for trading shifts rather than making side deals.

Talking with your manager, by text and email

Messages to a manager aren't the same as messages to friends. Keep them short, clear and polite:

  • Say who you are. Your manager may not have your number saved. Start with your name and, at a bigger workplace, your department.
  • Get to the point. What's happening, what you need, and what you'll do next.
  • Use full words and normal punctuation. No slang, no all caps, and skip the emojis unless your manager uses them first.
  • For email, add a clear subject line, such as "Time-off request: Saturday the 10th," plus a greeting and your name at the end.

Also think about timing. Unless it's urgent, don't message your manager late at night. And when something isn't clear, such as a task, a schedule change or a rule, ask rather than guess.

Worked example

Malik is three weeks into a job bagging groceries. At 3:50 p.m. on a Tuesday he double-checks the schedule and realizes his shift started at 4:00, not 5:00. He's 25 minutes away. Here's how he handles it, step by step:

Taking feedback and handling mistakes

Feedback is information about how you're doing, and in a new job most of it will be about what to change. It can sting, but it's a manager investing time in you. When you get it:

  • Listen all the way through without interrupting or arguing.
  • Ask a question if you're not sure what they want: "So next time I should check the date before shelving the milk?"
  • Say thanks, then actually do it differently. Showing the change is what counts.

You will make mistakes. Everyone does. The steps are the same as Malik's: tell the right person quickly, own it without excuses, help fix it, and change something so it doesn't happen again. A mistake you report yourself is usually forgiven. A mistake someone else discovers you hid usually isn't.

Common mistake

Hiding a mistake and hoping nobody notices, like quietly throwing away something you broke or leaving a cash count you know is wrong. It feels safer in the moment, but it turns a small, fixable problem into a question of whether you can be trusted.

Your rights at work

Being a good employee doesn't mean accepting anything. In general terms, you have a right to:

  • A safe workplace. You should get training on the safety risks of your job. If something seems dangerous, say so. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA.gov) explains workers' safety rights.
  • Be treated without harassment or discrimination. Unwanted comments, touching, threats or unfair treatment because of who you are are not "part of the job." The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC.gov) explains these rights.
  • Be paid for the time you work. Keep your own record of your hours and compare it with your pay stub (Money & Business Foundations lesson 2 shows how to read one).

Federal law, the Fair Labor Standards Act, sets limits on the hours and kinds of work for workers under 18. Beyond that, rules for teen workers, such as work permits, hours limits and minimum wage, vary by state; check your state's labor department. The US Department of Labor (DOL.gov) is another good source.

If something feels wrong, tell a manager you trust, and tell a parent or another trusted adult. Laws generally protect workers from being punished for reporting a safety problem or harassment. This lesson is a general guide, not legal advice.

Key idea

Show up, communicate early and clearly, take feedback without arguing, and own your mistakes. And remember that being a reliable worker never means accepting unsafe or unfair treatment.

Comparing jobs and offers

The job that pays the most per hour isn't always the one that pays you the most for your time.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • List what to compare in a job besides the hourly rate.
  • Work out your real hourly pay once commute time and cost are counted.
  • Ask good questions before you accept, and turn down an offer politely.

An offer is more than a number

A job offer is an employer saying "we'd like to hire you," usually with a pay rate, a start date and some idea of your hours. It's tempting to look at the hourly wage and stop there. But the wage is only one part of what a job gives you and takes from you.

When you compare jobs, look at five things: how much you'll earn in a normal week, when you'll work, what it costs you in time and money to get there, what you'll learn, and where the job could lead. A job that looks worse on the first can win on the other four.

All the dollar amounts in this lesson are made-up example numbers, not claims about what any job pays. And they're all before taxes; Money & Business Foundations lesson 2 explains what comes out of a paycheck.

Hours and schedule

Weekly pay is the hourly wage times the hours you actually work. A high wage with only six hours a week can earn less than a lower wage with fifteen. So ask how many hours a new employee usually gets, not just the most you could get. "Up to 25 hours" might mean 8.

Then ask when those hours are. Does the schedule fit around school, sports and homework? Is it posted far enough ahead that you can plan? Are there late closing shifts on school nights? A job that keeps changing your schedule at the last minute can cost you sleep and grades, which don't show up on a pay stub.

Remember that rules for teen workers, including how many hours and how late you can work, vary by state; check your state's labor department.

The hidden cost: getting there

Your commute, the trip to and from work, costs two things: money (gas, parking, bus or train fares) and time. Both come out of what the job is really worth to you.

You can put all of that into one number, your real hourly pay:

  • Weekly pay = hourly wage × hours worked per week.
  • Subtract what you spend on the commute each week.
  • Divide by all the hours the job takes: hours worked plus hours spent commuting.

That tells you what you earn for every hour the job takes out of your week, not just the hours on the clock. Try the calculator below with a job you have or one you're thinking about.

Training, growth and the things money doesn't show

Especially at a first job, what you learn can matter as much as what you earn. Compare:

  • Training. Will someone actually teach you, or will you be thrown in on day one?
  • Skills. Will you handle money, talk with customers, use equipment, or help train others later? Those skills go on your next resume.
  • Growth. Do people get raises or become shift leads? Ask how long current leads have been there.
  • People and safety. Did the manager seem respectful and organized in the interview? Did the workplace seem safe?
  • Extras. Some jobs offer an employee discount, free meals on shift or other benefits. They're nice, but count them only if you'd really use them.

Common mistake

Choosing the job with the higher hourly rate without asking about hours, schedule or the commute. A higher rate can mean fewer hours, late shifts on school nights, or a long, expensive trip that quietly shrinks your real pay.

Asking questions, then saying yes or no

You're allowed to ask questions before you accept. Polite, useful ones include: "How many hours a week do new employees usually get?", "How far ahead is the schedule posted?", "What does training look like?" and "When would I start?"

It's also fine to ask for a short time to decide: "Thank you so much. Could I let you know by Friday?" Then reply when you said you would. Ask for the pay rate, start date and schedule in writing, such as an email, so there's no confusion later.

If you turn an offer down, do it quickly and kindly: "Thank you for the offer. I've decided to accept another position, but I really appreciated meeting you." You may want to apply there again someday.

Key idea

Compare jobs by what they give you for all the time they take: count commute time and cost, look at hours, schedule and growth, and then decide.

Growing your career

Your first job won't be your last, and what you do while you're there shapes every job that comes after it.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Build skills and a network of people who can vouch for you.
  • Keep a record of your accomplishments and use it to earn strong references.
  • Leave a job well and weigh your next steps after high school.

Keep building skills

A career is built one skill at a time, and a part-time job is a good place to start. Once you're comfortable with your main tasks, ask for more: "Could you show me how to close out the register?" or "I'd like to learn the ordering system if you ever need help." Managers remember the people who ask.

Skills also come from outside work. Clubs, sports, volunteering and school projects teach teamwork, leadership and organization. Some jobs value a certification, such as CPR and first aid for lifeguarding or babysitting, and many are short courses. Free online lessons (like this one) and your school counselor can point you to more.

Pay attention to what you enjoy and what you're good at. That's the most useful information you'll get for choosing what comes next.

Networking, done respectfully

Networking just means building relationships with people who know about work you might want. You already have a network: teachers, coaches, coworkers, managers, neighbors, family friends, and your friends' parents.

  • Be curious, not pushy. Ask people about their work: "What do you like about being an electrician? How did you get started?" Most people enjoy talking about what they do.
  • Respect their time. Ask for 15 minutes, show up on time, and come with a few questions.
  • Say thank you, in person and with a short message afterward.
  • Stay in touch now and then, not only when you need something, and help others when you can.

Stay safe while you do it. Meet new people in public places or at school events, involve a parent or trusted adult, and be careful what personal information you share online, including on a professional networking site. A mentor, someone more experienced who gives you advice over time, often grows out of exactly these conversations.

References, recommendations and your record of wins

Your managers and teachers are the people future employers and schools will ask about you. A reference is someone who answers questions about you; a letter of recommendation is a written letter vouching for you, often asked for by colleges, scholarships and programs.

Make it easy for them. Always ask before listing someone, give them plenty of notice (a couple of weeks for a letter, if you can), and tell them what it's for. Remind them of specific things you did. That's where an accomplishment log helps: a simple running note where you jot down wins as they happen, like praise from a customer, a new skill, or a problem you solved. You'll forget most of them otherwise.

Worked example

Tyler worked at a hardware store for 18 months, from January of his junior year to July after he graduated, and kept an accomplishment log in his phone's notes. Now he's leaving to start a trade school program in electrical work. Here's how he turns his notes into something useful:

What he jotted downHow he uses it

Step by step, Tyler (1) tells his manager in person that he's leaving and gives two weeks' notice; (2) asks whether the manager would be a reference, and sends a short list of the log entries above; (3) works his last two weeks as hard as his first; and (4) sends a thank-you note on his last day. His manager now has specific things to say, and the door is open if Tyler wants summer work later.

Leaving a job well

People leave jobs all the time: for school, a better offer, a move or a schedule that no longer works. How you leave is what your manager remembers.

  • Tell your manager first, in person or by phone if you can, before you tell coworkers.
  • Give notice. Two weeks' notice is the usual custom in the US. Check your employee handbook in case your workplace asks for something different.
  • Keep it positive. You don't need to list complaints. "I'm starting a program in the fall and need to focus on it" is enough.
  • Finish strong. Show up for every remaining shift, help train your replacement if asked, and return uniforms, keys or badges.

If you're leaving because something is unsafe or you're being harassed, your safety comes first; talk to a parent or trusted adult, and see Lesson 4.

Common mistake

Quitting by simply not showing up, or by sending an angry text on the way out. It can feel satisfying for a minute, but you lose a reference, and in a small town or a close-knit field, people talk. Leave the way you'd want to be remembered.

What comes next, and how far you've come

After high school there's no single right path, and many people combine or switch between these:

  • College: a two-year community college or a four-year college or university.
  • Trade school: focused training for skilled jobs such as electrician, welder, medical assistant or auto technician, often in two years or less.
  • Apprenticeship: paid, on-the-job training with classroom learning, so you earn while you learn a trade.
  • Military: service that includes training in a specific job; talk with your family, a counselor and more than one recruiter before deciding.
  • Work: going straight into a job and building skills there, possibly with school later.

That ends this course. You've worked out what kinds of jobs fit you, built a resume, practiced interview stories, learned how to be someone a manager can count on, compared offers by real hourly pay, and planned how to grow from here. Keep your accomplishment log going, and keep asking good questions.

Key idea

Treat every job as a step: learn all you can, keep a record of what you do well, treat people well so they'll vouch for you, and leave in a way that keeps doors open.

What marketing really is

Marketing isn't tricking people into buying things. It's figuring out what people need and making it easy for them to get it.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain why marketing is about creating value for customers, not just running ads.
  • Tell the difference between a customer's need and their want, and a feature and a benefit.
  • Use the four Ps to describe how any business reaches its customers.

More than ads

When most people hear "marketing", they picture ads: a video before the one you wanted to watch, a sign by the road, a post from someone paid to show off a product. Ads are part of marketing, but only a small part.

Marketing is everything a business does to understand what people want, offer something that meets it, and get it into their hands. That includes deciding what to make, what to charge, where to sell it and how to tell people about it.

At its heart is value: what a customer gets compared with what they give up. They give up money, but also time and effort. People buy when what they get feels worth more than what it costs them. Good marketing creates that value for real; bad marketing just claims it.

Needs, wants, and the problem underneath

You may have met needs and wants in budgeting. Marketing uses the same words from the customer's side:

  • A need is the basic thing a person is missing: something to eat, a way to get to school, help passing a class, a way to feel like they fit in.
  • A want is the particular way they'd like to meet that need: a burrito from the food truck, a ride with a friend, a tutor who explains things slowly.

Businesses don't create needs. They offer better ways to meet the ones people already have. So look underneath what people ask for. A parent who hires a tutor doesn't really want "an hour of algebra". They want their kid less stressed and passing the class.

That's why marketers separate features from benefits. A feature is what a product is or has: "one-hour sessions, twice a week." A benefit is what it does for the customer: "walk into your test feeling ready." People buy benefits; features are the proof.

The four Ps

A handy way to organize marketing decisions is the four Ps, sometimes called the marketing mix:

  • Product: what you offer. It can be a thing, like a bracelet, or a service, like tutoring. It includes the details: quality, size, options, how it's packaged, what happens if something goes wrong.
  • Price: what the customer pays, and how. One price or several? A discount for regulars? Paid before or after? (For how to set a price that covers your costs, see "How a business makes money" in Money & Business Foundations.)
  • Place: where and when customers can get it. A stand at a school event, a website, delivery to their door, or only on weekends.
  • Promotion: how people find out about it and why they'd care. Flyers, word of mouth, posts, a table at an event, a demo.

Every business, from a lemonade stand to a car company, makes a choice about each P, even if it never uses the words.

Worked example

Aaliyah loves dogs and notices that several neighbors on her street work long shifts. She asks three of them what's hard about having a dog, and hears the same thing each time: the dog is stuck inside all afternoon, and the owner feels guilty. That's the need. With her parents' OK, she plans a dog-walking service and walks through the four Ps:

PAaliyah's decisionWhy it fits her customers
ProductA 30-minute walk, fresh water, and a photo texted to the owner after every walkOwners want to know their dog is OK, not just that it was walked
Price$12 a walk, or $50 for a pack of five walksClose to what other walkers nearby charge; the pack rewards regular customers
PlaceWeekday afternoons and Saturday mornings, only on streets within a 10-minute walk of homeShe can go straight from school, and staying close means no rides needed
PromotionA flyer on the neighborhood bulletin board, and asking happy customers to tell a neighborHer customers all live within a few blocks, so local beats wide

The Ps have to fit together

The four Ps work as a set. If one doesn't match the others, customers notice. A handmade, high-quality product sold at a bargain-bin price makes people wonder what's wrong with it. A great service offered only at times your customers are busy won't get booked. A flyer promising "same-day delivery" is a problem if you can only deliver on weekends.

When you change one P, check the other three. If Aaliyah started taking dogs to a park across town (place), she'd need more time per walk (product), might need to charge more (price) and would have to reach owners who've never seen her flyer (promotion).

Common mistake

Thinking good promotion can sell anything. Promotion only tells people about what you offer. If the product doesn't meet a real need, or the price and place don't work for customers, more promotion just helps more people discover that it isn't for them. Fix the offer first; then tell people about it.

Marketing is a promise you have to keep

Every piece of marketing makes a promise: this will taste good, this will arrive on time, this walk will leave your dog happy. The sale is only the start. If you keep the promise, customers come back and tell friends. If you break it, no amount of advertising will win them back.

That's why the best marketers spend most of their time listening to customers and improving what they offer, not polishing slogans. The rest of this course builds on that: knowing your customer, shaping your message, spreading the word, measuring what works, and selling honestly.

Key idea

Marketing starts with a customer's need, not an ad. Get the product, price, place and promotion to fit that need and each other, then keep the promise you make.

Knowing your customer

If you try to sell to everyone, you end up talking to no one, so start by learning exactly who you're serving.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Choose a target market and split a market into segments.
  • Build a simple customer persona from real information.
  • Run small surveys, interviews and observations, and write questions that don't push people toward an answer.

Not everyone is your customer

It's tempting to say "my product is for everyone." It almost never is. A lawn-care service is no use to someone in an apartment. A study guide for a chemistry class doesn't help a student who isn't taking chemistry.

Your target market is the specific group of people most likely to need what you offer and be able to buy it. Picking one isn't about shutting people out. Anyone can still buy from you. It's about deciding who you design for, where you show up and how you talk, so your limited time and money go where they'll count.

Segments: splitting a market into groups

A market is usually made of smaller groups with different needs. Each one is a segment. Common ways to split a market:

  • Who they are: age, stage of life (student, new parent, retired), household size.
  • Where they are: neighborhood, school, town, or how far they'll travel.
  • What they do: how often they buy, what they use now, whether they do it themselves.
  • What they care about: lowest price, speed, quality, convenience, doing something good.

The last two often matter most. Two neighbors of the same age can want completely different things: one wants the cheapest option, the other will pay extra to never think about it again.

Customer personas

A customer persona is a short, made-up profile of one typical customer in a segment, built from what you've actually learned. It gives the group a face, so you can ask, "Would this work for her?"

A good persona includes a first name, their situation, the problem they have, what they do about it now, and what would make them switch. Keep it grounded in real answers you heard, not guesses. A persona built on stereotypes ("teenagers only care about looks") will steer you wrong, and it isn't fair to the people it describes.

Simple research: ask, listen, watch

Market research just means gathering information about customers before you guess. Three easy methods:

  • Surveys: the same few questions to many people. Good for counting: how many do X? Keep it short, five questions or fewer.
  • Interviews: a real conversation with a handful of people. Good for learning why. Ask them to tell you about the last time they had the problem.
  • Watching behavior: noticing what people actually do. Which table at the school fair has a line? What do people buy when the snack stand runs out of their first choice?

You can also look at what already exists: what similar businesses offer, what they charge, and what their customers praise or complain about in public reviews.

What people say and what they do don't always match. Plenty of people say they'd buy something and then don't. So trust past behavior over future promises. Stay safe, too: survey people you know or with an adult nearby, and don't collect personal details you don't need.

Worked example

Asking questions that don't push

The way you ask can change the answer. A leading question nudges people toward the answer you're hoping for, and people are often too polite to disagree. Compare:

  • Leading: "Wouldn't it be great to never mow again?" Better: "How do you feel about mowing your lawn?"
  • Leading: "Would you buy my amazing cookies?" Better: "When did you last buy a snack at school? What did you get?"
  • Leading: "Most people hate cooking, right?" Better: "What do you usually do for dinner on busy nights?"

Use open-ended questions that can't be answered with yes or no, ask about real past events instead of "would you ever", and don't pitch your idea while you're researching. Your goal is to learn, not to be told your idea is good.

Common mistake

Only asking your friends and family. They like you and want you to succeed, so they'll often say yes to an idea they'd never pay for. Their answers are a start, but talk to people who fit your target market and don't know you well, and pay more attention to what they've done than to what they say they might do.

Key idea

Pick a specific group to serve, learn about them by asking fair questions and watching what they do, and let what you learn, not what you hope, shape your plans.

Brand and message

Your brand isn't your logo. It's what people think of when they hear your name, and you build it with every promise you keep.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Decide how you want customers to see you compared with their other choices.
  • Write a value proposition and a one-sentence pitch.
  • Keep your name, look and voice consistent, and tell a story that's true.

What a brand really is

A brand is the set of ideas and feelings people connect with your business. It's what comes to mind when a friend says your name: reliable or flaky, fun or serious, worth the money or not.

A logo and colors help people recognize you, but they don't create the brand on their own. Every experience does: how fast you reply to messages, whether the order arrives when you said, how you handle a mistake. You can shape your brand, but customers decide what it is, based on what you actually do.

Positioning: your spot in the customer's mind

Customers always have other choices. Some are competitors, other businesses selling something similar. Others are simpler: doing it themselves, borrowing, or doing nothing at all.

Positioning is deciding what you want to be known for compared with those choices. You can't be the cheapest, the fastest, the highest quality and the most personal all at once. Pick the one or two things that matter most to your target market and that you can truly deliver, then build around them.

A simple test: finish the sentence "For people who want ___, we're the one that ___." If your answer could describe every other option, you haven't found your position yet.

Value proposition and one-sentence pitch

Your value proposition is the main reason a specific customer should choose you. A clear one answers three questions:

  • Who is it for? Your target market, from lesson 2.
  • What does it do for them? The benefit, not just the features.
  • Why you instead of the other choices? What makes you different, and the proof.

Then boil it down to a one-sentence pitch, sometimes called an elevator pitch: something you could say out loud to a stranger in about ten seconds. A useful pattern is "I help [who] [get what benefit] by [how you're different]." Skip the buzzwords. If a friend's little brother couldn't repeat it back, simplify it.

Worked example

Camila designs and prints custom vinyl stickers. She's been selling a few to friends, but when people ask what she does, she rambles. She works through her message step by step:

StepCamila's answer
1. Who is it for?School clubs and teams that want stickers of their own design
2. What do they use now?Generic stickers from a store, printing on paper at home, or ordering online, where many sellers want a large minimum order
3. What's the benefit?Stickers that show off their own club, without ordering hundreds they don't need
4. What makes her different?Small batches starting at 10, designed together in person at school, ready within a week
5. What's the proof?Photos of batches she's made for two clubs, and a sample sheet people can hold

Her pitch: "I make small batches of custom stickers for school clubs and teams, so you can get your own design without ordering hundreds." It's 22 words and easy to repeat.

She almost added "waterproof", but she'd only tested hand-washing a water bottle a few times. So she says "holds up to hand-washing", which is what she can back up. And she only prints designs the club made or has permission to use; the school's logo, for example, needs the school's OK.

Name, look and voice: stay consistent

People trust what they recognize. Consistency means your business looks and sounds the same everywhere: on a flyer, a price sheet, a table sign and your messages.

  • Name: easy to say, spell and remember. Make sure another business nearby isn't already using it, and never copy another business's name or logo. It confuses customers and can get you into legal trouble.
  • Look: a simple logo, the same two or three colors and one or two fonts. Simple is easier to recognize and cheaper to print.
  • Voice: your brand voice is the personality in your words. Friendly and playful? Calm and professional? Choose one and use it every time.

Write your choices on one page, with your colors, fonts, a few words that describe your voice and your pitch, so anyone helping you keeps it consistent too.

Common mistake

Trying to promise everything: "the cheapest, the best quality and the fastest!" Customers don't believe it, and you can't deliver it all. It also makes you sound like everyone else. Pick what you're truly best at for your customers and say that clearly.

Tell a true story

People like buying from people. A short, honest story about why you started, like "I made stickers for my own robotics team and other clubs started asking", makes you memorable and easy to talk about.

The story has to be true. Don't invent a "family recipe" that isn't one, claim years of experience you don't have, or post reviews from customers who don't exist. Only claim what you can back up, like Camila and her stickers. False claims can break the law, and once customers catch one, they doubt everything else you say. Lesson 6 covers the rules for honest marketing in more detail.

Key idea

Choose what you want to be known for, say it in one clear sentence, look and sound the same everywhere, and only make promises you can keep.

Lesson 4 of 6 · about 15 minutes

Getting the word out

The best product in town earns nothing if the people who need it never hear about it.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Name the main marketing channels and what each one is good for.
  • Create content that helps people instead of just shouting "buy now".
  • Pick the two or three channels that actually reach your audience.

Start with where your customers already are

A marketing channel is any path your message takes to reach people: a conversation, a flyer, a post, an email, an event. In lesson 3 you worked out what to say. This lesson is about how it gets to the right people.

Don't start by asking "which channel is coolest?" or "which one do I already use?" Start with the customer you described in lesson 2 and ask: where do they already look, listen and hang out? A retiree who wants their gutters cleaned and a ninth grader who wants custom stickers live in different places, online and off. Go to them; don't wait for them to come to you.

Channels close to home

For a small business, the cheapest channels are often the strongest:

  • Word of mouth is people telling other people. It's powerful because it comes from someone the listener already trusts. You can't buy it, but you can earn it by doing great work, and you can encourage it by asking happy customers, "Do you know anyone else who might want this?"
  • Referrals are word of mouth with a nudge: a small thank-you, like a discount on the next order, for a customer who sends a friend your way.
  • Flyers and posters work well for local services. Ask permission before posting on a community board or in a shop window, and follow any neighborhood rules. Never put flyers inside mailboxes; in the US, mailboxes are for postal mail only.
  • Events such as school fairs, craft markets and club nights let people see, taste or try what you offer, and ask you questions face to face.
  • Partnerships team you up with someone who serves the same people without competing with you. A student who bakes birthday cakes and a student who makes party decorations can recommend each other. That's called cross-promotion.

Social media, email and paid ads

Social media lets you show your work to people beyond your own street: photos on a social media app, short how-to clips on a video site. It's free to post, but it takes steady effort, and only some of your followers will see any single post.

An email list is one of the few channels you control yourself. People who sign up have said "yes, tell me more", so an email newsletter usually reaches people who are already interested. Only add people who have given you permission; lesson 6 covers the rules.

Paid ads put your message in front of people you pay to reach, on a website, an app, a local paper or a sign. They can reach people quickly, and many let you choose who sees them by age, location or interests. But they cost money every time, and a bad ad just burns cash. If you try one, start with a small amount and measure the results. Lesson 5 shows you how.

Content that helps

Most people tune out messages that only say "buy from me". They pay attention to things that are useful, interesting or fun. Helpful content teaches or shows something your customer cares about: a tutor's five-minute study tip, a baker's video on how to tell when cookies are done, a sticker maker's time-lapse of a design coming together.

Helpful content builds trust, because it shows you know your stuff before anyone spends a dollar. People also share useful things, which turns content into word of mouth. End each piece with a call to action: one clear next step, like "order by Friday" or "reply for a free quote". Without one, people enjoy your post and move on.

Common mistake

Marketing where you are instead of where your customers are. It's tempting to post only on the app your friends use, because it's easy and they'll like it. But likes from friends who will never buy don't grow a business. Check every channel against one question: does my target customer actually see this?

Match the channel to the audience, and keep it small

Each channel has strengths. Face-to-face and word of mouth build trust. Flyers and door hangers are good for customers who live nearby. Social media and video are good for showing what something looks like. Email is good for people who already know you. Paid ads are good for reaching many new people quickly, at a price.

You don't need all of them. Pick two or three that fit your customer, do them well and keep your message consistent across all of them. People often need to hear about something more than once before they act, so showing up regularly in a few places beats appearing once everywhere.

Lesson 5 of 6 · about 16 minutes

Measuring what works

Marketing without measuring is guessing, and guessing gets expensive fast.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Follow a promotion from the people it reached to the people who bought.
  • Calculate response rate, conversion rate and cost per new customer.
  • Run small, fair tests to find out which promotion really works better.

Why measure at all?

In lesson 4 you picked a few channels. Now comes the honest question: which ones actually brought in customers? A promotion can feel like a hit because lots of people saw it or said nice things, and still bring in almost no sales. Another can feel quiet and turn out to be your best source of customers.

Measuring means deciding what you want before you start, usually new customers, then counting what really happened. It lets you put your time and money where they work and stop doing what doesn't. You don't need special software: a notebook or a simple spreadsheet is enough.

From reach to customers

Every promotion loses people at each step. Picture it as a funnel, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom:

  • Reach is how many people saw or heard your message: people who took a flyer, walked past your table or were shown your post.
  • Responded is how many took the next step you asked for: clicked a link, scanned a code, signed up, texted for a quote. Online, this is often called clicks.
  • Bought is how many became new customers.

It's normal for each step to be much smaller than the one before. The numbers tell you where people drop off. If lots of people respond but few buy, the promotion is working and something after it isn't, like the price, the order form or your reply time. If hardly anyone responds, the message or the channel needs work.

Three numbers to calculate

Turn the funnel into three numbers you can compare across promotions:

  • Response rate = people who responded ÷ people reached. It shows how well the message grabbed attention.
  • Conversion rate = people who bought ÷ people who responded. It shows how well you turned interest into sales.
  • Cost per new customer = amount spent ÷ people who bought. It shows what each new customer cost you in marketing.

Multiply a rate by 100 to write it as a percent. Cost per new customer is often the most useful of the three, because it answers the real question: is this worth the money? Try the calculator below with any promotion you can think of.

Common mistake

Judging a promotion by how big it looks. Reach, views and likes are easy to count and feel good, but they don't pay for anything. Numbers like these, that look impressive without telling you whether the business is growing, are called vanity metrics. Always follow the funnel down to the customers who bought, and what each one cost.

Know where each customer came from

None of these numbers work unless you can connect each sale to the promotion that caused it. A few simple ways:

  • Give each promotion its own tracking code: a discount code, a separate order link or a different phone number to text.
  • Ask every new customer, "How did you hear about us?" and write down the answer.
  • Keep a simple table: promotion, amount spent, reach, responses, customers.

Write down what you spent too, including things like printing and free samples. Your time is a cost as well, even when no money changes hands.

Small tests, fair comparisons

Before you spend a lot, try a little. An A/B test compares two versions that differ in only one thing, such as the headline, the photo or the offer, shown to similar people at a similar time. If you change the photo and the price and the channel all at once, you can't tell which change made the difference.

Be careful with small numbers. Mei's 9 against 8 customers is a useful hint, not proof; a few people either way could flip the result. Run a test again, or run it longer, before making a big decision. Then keep what works, drop what doesn't and test the next idea.

Finally, remember that the first sale is only the start. A new customer who comes back again is worth more than one sale. If each of Mei's 8 craft-fair customers orders once more, she gets 16 sales from that $24, just $1.50 of marketing per sale. Lesson 6 is about earning those repeat customers.

Key idea

Follow every promotion from reach to responses to customers, compare cost per new customer, and change one thing at a time so your tests tell you something true.

Selling, service and honest marketing

Marketing brings people to your door; how you listen, treat them and tell the truth decides whether they ever come back.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Sell by asking questions and listening, and handle "no" with respect.
  • Turn good service and honest reviews into repeat customers.
  • Follow the basics of honest marketing: truthful claims, disclosed endorsements and permission before you email or text.

Selling is mostly listening

A lot of people picture selling as talking fast and pushing hard. Good selling is closer to the opposite. It means finding out what someone needs and then showing, honestly, whether what you offer fits.

Start with open questions, the kind that can't be answered with just yes or no: "What's been hardest about this?" "What have you tried already?" Then practice active listening: pay full attention, don't interrupt, and repeat back what you heard ("So the problem is less the homework and more the tests?"). People can tell when you're listening, and they trust you more for it.

Only then explain how you can help, in terms of their problem, not a list of everything you do. And if your product isn't a good fit, say so. Pointing someone elsewhere costs you one sale but earns you a reputation.

Handling "no"

You will hear "no" far more often than "yes", even with a great product. That's normal, and it isn't personal. A reason someone gives for not buying is called an objection, and it usually falls into a few groups:

  • Price: "It's too expensive." Ask what they're comparing it with, explain what's included, or offer a smaller option if you have one.
  • Timing: "Not right now." Ask if you can check back later, and then actually do it, once.
  • Trust: "I'm not sure it'll work." Offer proof: a sample, a trial, examples of your work or honest reviews.

Listen to the objection fully before you answer. And when someone really means no, accept it politely and leave the door open: "No problem. Thanks for your time." Pressure, guilt and fake deadlines ("only today!") might win one sale, but they lose the customer and everyone they tell.

Service, reviews and repeat customers

Customer service is everything that happens after the sale: showing up on time, answering messages, fixing mistakes. When something goes wrong, respond quickly, apologize without excuses and make it right. Handled well, a problem can leave a customer trusting you more than before.

Reviews are word of mouth that strangers can read. Ask happy customers for an honest review. Never write fake reviews, ask friends to pose as customers, or offer a reward only for positive ones. When a bad review comes in, reply calmly, thank them, and explain how you're fixing it; future customers read your reply too.

A repeat customer is someone who buys from you again. As you saw in lesson 5, winning a new customer costs money and effort; someone who comes back doesn't need another ad to find you. Remember names and preferences, check in after the sale and keep your promises.

Common mistake

Stretching the truth to close a sale: "Guaranteed A's!", "Everyone loves it!", "Only two spots left!" when that isn't true. It might work once. Then the customer finds out, asks for their money back, and tells others. Only promise what you can actually deliver.

Honest marketing: truth, disclosure and permission

Honesty isn't just good manners; it's also the law. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC.gov) says advertising must be truthful and not misleading. A few basics for any business, however small:

  • Tell the truth in ads. Don't claim results you can't back up, hide important details or use fake "before" photos.
  • Disclose endorsements. If you pay someone, or give them free products, to talk about your business, they need to say so clearly. The same applies to you if a company ever sends you free stuff to post about.
  • Get permission before you email or text. Rules on marketing emails and texts require things like consent or an easy way to opt out. Only message people who agreed to hear from you, make it easy to unsubscribe in every message, and stop right away when someone asks.
  • Protect people's information. Collect only what you need, keep it private and never sell or share your customer list.

This is a starting point, not legal advice. The simple version: follow the rules, get permission and never say anything you wouldn't be comfortable having checked.
